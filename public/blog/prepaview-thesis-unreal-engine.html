CASE STUDY

How Three Students Built a Game in Unreal Engine — And Almost Didn't Graduate

March 2026 · 5 min read

Somewhere between "this is due in a year" and "this is due in a month," our thesis stopped being a project and became a test of endurance.

Prepaview started in late 2025 as a three-person team. I was the leader and the one building the game in Unreal Engine. A classmate handled co-development — the helper who caught the bugs I missed. Our third teammate owned the documentation, writing the thesis paper that would eventually face our panel.

The plan was simple: build a high-fidelity gameplay prototype with responsive controls and believable AI, then write the paper around it.

The execution was not.

The Stall — December 2025

By December 2025 we had stalled. My co-developer and I were plateauing — real life had a way of showing up right when the semester demanded everything. The project sat unfinished, the bugs piled up, and the deadline didn't care.

The Lock-In — January to March 2026

So we made the only decision that made sense: we locked in for the final month and reworked the entire project.

The rework was brutal. Features we thought were done were held together by duct tape. AI systems misbehaved. Movement felt off. Every fix uncovered two more problems. We threw out what didn't work, rebuilt what did, and kept the parts worth keeping.

The Final Week — March 2026

Then came the final week. I stopped sleeping properly. I put every waking hour I had into Prepaview — fixing crashes, tuning player control, getting the AI to behave, polishing the build. My teammates held down the documentation and kept the thesis moving. We worked like the deadline was a physical wall closing in, because it was.

On the last day, it worked. We built the Android build, published it, and handed in a thesis we were proud of. Not because it was perfect — it wasn't. But because three students, a pile of bugs, and a deadline that almost won got shipped anyway.

What I Learned

  1. Roles matter more than titles. We had a leader, a builder, and a documenter — and we needed all three. When I was heads-down in the engine, my teammates kept the thesis alive. Nobody could do it alone.
  2. Progress isn't linear. We plateaued for months, then compressed a semester's worth of work into one month. Sometimes a project needs time to stew before you can see what's actually wrong with it.
  3. The last 10% is the real work. The game worked at 80% for weeks. The final stretch — the bugs, the polish, the Android build — is where almost everything broke. That's the part you can't skip.
  4. Ship, even if it's ugly. We published with rough edges. It still shipped. A thesis on paper is a requirement; a thesis that runs on a phone is a story.

What's Next

Prepaview is done — but the lessons aren't. The discipline I found in that final month is the same discipline I used to build FrogPOS. If you're in the middle of your own final month: keep going. The other side is worth it.
